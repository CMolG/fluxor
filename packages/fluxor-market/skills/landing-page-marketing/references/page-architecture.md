# Page architecture

How to choose a landing page's sections, their order and its length, and how to build the first
screen. Evidence grades: **[PR]** peer-reviewed · **[LS]** large-sample industry data (usually
correlational) · **[CS]** single case · **[PO]** practitioner framework. Figures are as reported by
the named source; re-check the primary source before quoting one to a client.

## 1. Principles that decide structure

- **People scan first and read second.** In NN/g's classic study 79% of users scanned a new page and
  only 16% read word by word (NN/g 1997, [LS]). In eyetracking, the F-pattern is what happens when
  text is an unformatted wall. Design for *layer-cake* scanning (reading headings only) and *spotted*
  scanning (hunting for numbers and keywords): headings that carry the message on their own, key
  numbers visible, one idea per paragraph (NN/g 2017).
- **The first screen decides whether anything else is read.** About 57% of viewing time was above
  the fold and 74% within the first two screens (NN/g 2018 eyetracking, [LS]). Most exits happen in
  the first 10–20 seconds; pages that hold people for about 30 seconds tend to keep them (NN/g,
  after Liu, White & Dumais 2010, [LS]). People do scroll, but only when the top earns it. Both "the
  fold doesn't matter" and "nobody scrolls" are myths.
- **Clarity, then relevance, then value, then differentiation, then friction.** Wynter's message
  layers ([PO]) give the order in which a visitor's questions must be answered: "I get it" →
  "it's for me" → "I want that" → "I see why this one" → "nothing's stopping me".
- **Purchase = desire − (labor + confusion)** (Julian Shapiro, [PO]). Every block either raises
  desire (value, proof) or lowers labor and confusion. If it does neither, cut it.
- **One page, one goal.** One reader, one big idea, one offer, one primary CTA (the "Rule of One",
  from Mark Ford/Michael Masterson, popularised by Copyhackers, [PO]). Secondary links (nav, footer)
  shouldn't compete with the primary action.
- **Familiar beats novel for structure.** Visitors know where to look on a conventional landing
  page, so be original in the message, not the layout (Shapiro, [PO]).

## 2. The first screen (hero)

The first screen must pass the **5-second test** on desktop *and* mobile: what it is, who it's for,
what I get, why believe it, what to do next (see `audit-rubric.md` §5). StoryBrand calls this the
"grunt test" ([PO]). The 5-second figure is a heuristic, not a measurement. The 50 ms "first
impression" study (Lindgaard et al. 2006, [PR]) is about visual appeal, not comprehension.

| Element | Job | Rules |
|---|---|---|
| **Headline** | The promise: the outcome for this reader | Specific, concrete, falsifiable. In customer language. Matches the ad or query that brought them. Say what you *do*, not "Welcome to X" or "Powered by AI" (NN/g 2025: technology isn't a value proposition). |
| **Sub-headline** | What it is + how it delivers / for whom | One or two sentences. Names the product category if the headline doesn't. Carries the mechanism ("…by building the rota from your staff's availability"). |
| **Primary CTA** | The next step, framed as what they get | "Start my free trial", "Get my first bag", not "Submit". |
| **Risk reducer** | Removes the fear of clicking | Right under the button: "14 days free · no card · cancel anytime". Only if true. |
| **Proof point** | The first reason to believe | Rating with review count, a named result, recognisable customer logos, "used by N" (only if accurate). |
| **Visual** | Shows the product working or the outcome | Real product UI, product photo in use, a before/after. Not stock mood imagery. |

Checks: no "false floor" (a full-bleed hero that looks like the end of the page). On mobile, the
headline, sub-headline and primary CTA fit in the first screen without overflow. No carousel (only
the first slide gets seen). Nav is minimal.

**CXL's value-proposition anatomy** ([PO]) is a compact version: a headline with the end benefit; a
sub-headline or short paragraph saying what it is, for whom and why it's useful; about 3 bullets of
key benefits; a visual. Peep Laja's fill-in check is "We help X do Y by doing Z." It's not a
headline, but if you can't fill it in, the headline won't be clear either.

## 3. The default section order

This is for solution-aware traffic and mid-priced products. Adjust it with §4–5. It is a synthesis
of Shapiro, StoryBrand, CXL/Wynter, Copyhackers, NN/g and Baymard.

| # | Section | Job (lever moved or objection answered) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Hero** | Clarity, outcome, first proof, next step (§2). |
| 2 | **Proof strip** | Likelihood: rating + count, logos, press, "used by" (accurate). |
| 3 | **Problem / stakes** | Makes the cost of the status quo felt, in customer words (PAS or before/after). Keep it short for problem-aware readers who already feel it. |
| 4 | **How it works (3 steps)** | Lowers perceived effort and time; makes the outcome imaginable. Include a labelled sample of what the product produces (an email it sends, a lesson list, what's in the box) when you don't have a real screenshot. |
| 5 | **Outcomes, each with the feature that causes it** | Outcome + mechanism + proof. 3–6 blocks, each also answering one objection, all tied back to the hero's promise. |
| 6 | **Deep proof** | Named, specific testimonials matched to the reader; case results; demo; ratings distribution. |
| 7 | **Differentiation and fit** | Why this instead of the alternatives (competitors, DIY, doing nothing), then a short "for you if… / not for you if…" block. Honest exclusions make the fit feel real to the right buyer. |
| 8 | **Offer and pricing** | Price framed against value and the real alternative; recommended plan; what's included; total cost. |
| 9 | **Risk reversal and trust** | Guarantee/trial/cancel terms, security, real company and support details. |
| 10 | **FAQ / objections** | The questions that stop purchases: price, time to value, switching, data, contract. |
| 11 | **Final CTA** | Restate the promise and the risk reducer; same CTA label as the hero. |
| 12 | **Footer** | Contact, policies, legal disclosures. |

On long mobile pages, a slim sticky CTA bar (price + button) that appears after the hero keeps the next step in reach. Hide it while the pricing section is on screen.

Put a CTA after every section that could plausibly be the point where someone is convinced
(usually after the proof, after pricing, and at the end), not only at the top and bottom.

## 4. Adjusting by awareness level

Eugene Schwartz's five levels (*Breakthrough Advertising*, 1966) describe what the visitor already
knows. The opening must start where they are ("enter the conversation already in the reader's
mind", Collier). Copyhackers' 10/90 idea ([PO]): the top ~10% of the page matches the awareness
stage and the traffic source; the other 90% persuades.

| Level | Typical traffic | Open with | Page shape |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Most aware**: knows the product, wants the deal | Retargeting, existing users, brand search, waitlist, email | The offer: product name, price, what's included, CTA | Short. Offer, key proof and terms, CTA. Real urgency only if it exists. |
| **Product-aware**: knows it, not convinced | Comparison search, "X vs Y", reviews sites, return visits | Differentiation and proof; the reason it beats what they're comparing | Medium. Comparison, proof and objections move up. |
| **Solution-aware**: knows the kind of solution, not yours | Category search ("staff scheduling app") | Your specific outcome + mechanism vs other solutions | Default order (§3). |
| **Problem-aware**: feels the pain, doesn't know solutions | Problem search ("clients paying late"), cold social, content | The problem in their words, then the outcome of solving it (PAS or before/after) | Longer. Education before price. A softer CTA (free tool, trial, sample) often fits. |
| **Unaware**: doesn't recognise the problem | Cold display/social, broad audiences | A story, surprising fact or identity hook that reveals the problem | Longest; reads like an article or advertorial; the pitch comes late. |

Also judge **market sophistication** (Schwartz's stages): how many similar promises the market has
already heard.

- **Stage 1**, a new category: state the claim plainly.
- **Stage 2**: enlarge or make the claim more specific.
- **Stage 3**, claims exhausted: introduce the **mechanism** (the new reason it works).
- **Stage 4**: elaborate the mechanism or make it more specific.
- **Stage 5**, a skeptical market: lead with identity and experience, not claims.

Crowded SaaS categories are usually stage 3–5. There, a new mechanism or a sharp position beats a
bigger promise.

## 5. Adjusting by price/risk, traffic source and page type

**Price and risk.**

- **Low-price or impulse:** short. Product, price, images, reviews and the buy button in the first
  screen, with shipping and returns right beside them.
- **High-ticket or B2B:** longer, with more proof (case studies, security and compliance, ROI math,
  a named person to talk to). "Book a demo" is often the CTA. Still show a "from" or typical price,
  because hiding prices raises distrust (NN/g "Show Price": price was the top-ranked B2B
  information need, [LS/usability]).

**Traffic source.**

- **Paid search:** strict message match with the keyword; lean page.
- **Paid social:** cold, so problem-first with more proof.
- **Email or retargeting:** offer-first.
- **Referral or partner:** lead with the referrer's endorsement.
- **Product Hunt / Hacker News:** show the product immediately and let people try it without friction.

**Page type.**

| Type | What carries the value | Must include |
|---|---|---|
| **SaaS / app** | Product visuals, how it works, time to first value | Real UI screenshots or a short demo; trial terms; integrations the buyer already uses; security basics. |
| **E-commerce product** | Images → reviews → specs → total cost and delivery date (Baymard's order, [LS]) | An in-scale image and in-use images; rating with count and distribution; shipping cost and delivery date near the price; returns policy by the buy button. |
| **Subscription (physical)** | The recurring outcome (freshness, convenience) and control | Per-unit/per-use price; what arrives when; how to pause, skip or cancel. |
| **Course / info product** | Transformation proof and the guarantee | Who it's for / not for; curriculum as outcomes; instructor credibility; typical results stated honestly (FTC typicality rules apply strictly); refund terms. |
| **Service / agency** | Authority, process and cases | Named case studies with numbers; the process in steps; who you are; how pricing works (ranges if custom). |
| **Dev tool / API** | Time to hello-world, docs, honesty | A code sample in the hero, a quickstart, docs and pricing links, open source/licence, no marketing fog. |

## 6. Length

Length follows **awareness × price/risk × complexity**. A page can't be too long, only too boring or
too hard to scan: every section must answer a real question. Short-page and long-page "always wins"
claims are myths. The famous Crazy Egg redesign (about 20× longer, part of a programme credited
with +363%) is a single case with many changes bundled together ([CS]). Ogilvy's version: long copy
sells when it's specific and relevant, and "the more you tell, the more you sell" applies to
considered purchases, not impulse buys.

## 7. CTAs and forms

- **One primary action**, repeated. Same label each time. A secondary, lower-commitment CTA ("See a
  2-minute demo") is fine if it doesn't compete visually.
- **Button labels say what they get**, first-person often reads well ("Start my free trial").
  Generic labels ("Get started", "Learn more") attract clicks but mislead people who wanted
  something specific (NN/g, [usability]).
- **Risk reducer under the button**, not in a footnote.
- **Forms:** ask only what the next step needs. Every field is effort. For self-serve products, an
  email (or SSO) is enough to start. If you must qualify leads, explain why you ask. Match the CTA
  to the sales model: don't force a demo booking on a $12/month self-serve product.
- **Contrast, not colour:** the "red button beats green" test was a single test on a green page,
  where the red button won by contrast ([CS]). Make the primary CTA the highest-contrast element
  in its area.

## 8. Visuals and layout

- **Show the product doing the job**, or the outcome. Real screenshots cropped to the relevant part,
  product photos in use, before/after, short looping demos. Photos make claims feel more true even
  when they prove nothing (Newman et al. 2012, [PR]). That's why they must be real.
- **Design quality is a credibility signal.** "Design look" was the most-mentioned credibility
  factor in Stanford's web credibility study (Fogg et al. 2003, n≈2,684, [LS]). Polish matters, but
  it can't replace clarity.
- **Readability:** body text ≥16px, line length about 50–75 characters, WCAG AA contrast (4.5:1).
  Low-contrast grey text is the most common accessibility failure (WebAIM Million, [LS]). Real text,
  not text inside images.
- **Speed is part of the offer.** Aim for LCP ≤2.5 s, INP ≤200 ms, CLS ≤0.1. In Google/Deloitte's
  37-brand study, 0.1 s faster mobile speed went with +8.4% retail conversions and +21.6% lead-gen
  form completion (2020, [LS], Google-commissioned, correlational). Portent (2022, [LS]) found 1-second
  pages converting about 3× better than 5-second pages. Compress the hero image, don't lazy-load it,
  reserve space for media to avoid layout shift, and keep scripts few.
- **Mobile first:** most traffic is mobile for consumer products. Check the mobile first screen
  separately. Stacked sections, tap targets ≥44px, no horizontal overflow.
