# The advertising canon, applied to landing pages

The principles behind the skill's rules, by source, and what each means for a page and for
perceived value. Read the source you need; you don't need all of it.

Quotes marked *(verified wording)* match across several independent sources. Those marked
*(paraphrase)* are the idea, not exact words. Don't put paraphrases in quotation marks when you
cite them to a user. Research findings carry evidence grades: **[PR]** peer-reviewed, **[LS]**
large-sample industry data, **[CS]** single case, **[PO]** practitioner opinion.

## 1. David Ogilvy: *Confessions of an Advertising Man* (1963), *Ogilvy on Advertising* (1983)

- **Research before writing.** Study the product until you find the fact that sells it. The
  Rolls-Royce headline ("At 60 miles an hour the loudest noise in this new Rolls-Royce comes from
  the electric clock") came from weeks of reading technical material, and the ad ran 719 words of
  facts. On a page: the value brief comes first, and the best line is usually a fact nobody else
  bothered to state.
- **Positioning is the first decision:** "what the product does, and who it is for". His Dove
  example: "I could have positioned Dove as a detergent bar for men with dirty hands, but chose
  instead to position it as a toilet bar for women with dry skin" *(verified wording)*. The
  positioning decides which value the visitor judges the product on.
- **The promise is the second decision.** "Unless your advertising contains a big idea, it will pass
  like a ship in the night" *(verified wording)*.
- **Headlines:**
  - "On the average, five times as many people read the headline as read the body copy. When you
    have written your headline, you have spent eighty cents out of your dollar" (*Confessions*,
    *verified wording*). This was a mid-century print heuristic, not web data, but the principle
    matches NN/g's findings on scanning.
  - Promise a benefit. Carry news. Be specific. Flag down the prospect.
  - Don't write riddles.
  - Longer headlines with news and information outsold short ones in the tests he cites.
- **Specific and factual body copy.** "Avoid superlatives, generalizations, and platitudes. Be
  specific and factual" *(verified wording)*. "Factual advertising like this outsells flatulent
  puffery. The more you tell, the more you sell" (on the Rolls-Royce ad) *(verified wording)*.
- **Long copy is fine when relevant.** "Readership falls off rapidly up to 50 words of copy, but drops
  very little between 50 and 500 words" *(verified wording)*.
- **Testimonials.** "The reader finds it easier to believe the endorsement of a fellow consumer than
  the puffery of an anonymous copywriter" *(verified wording)*. He was wary of celebrity endorsers,
  because people remember the celebrity and forget the product *(paraphrase)*.
- **Captions.** More people read captions under photos than body copy, so make every caption a
  small ad carrying the promise *(paraphrase of his claim)*. On a page: screenshot captions and
  image alt text should carry benefits, not labels.
- **Respect the reader.** "The consumer isn't a moron; she is your wife. You insult her intelligence
  if you assume that a mere slogan and a few vapid adjectives will persuade her to buy anything.
  She wants all the information you can give her" *(verified wording)*. This is the case against
  hype words and vague claims.
- **Sell, don't entertain.** "I do not regard advertising as entertainment or an art form, but as a
  medium of information" *(verified wording)*. "It's not creative unless it sells" is Benton &
  Bowles's motto, which Ogilvy quoted.
- **Test, and learn from direct response.** Direct mail was his "first love and secret weapon"
  (chapter title), because it measures what works.
- **The brand is built by every page.** A page that converts today but looks cheap can erode price
  perception later.

**For perceived value:** facts make value believable; positioning chooses the yardstick; a big
promise gives a reason to read; peers' testimonials move the claim from seller to buyer.

## 2. Claude Hopkins: *Scientific Advertising* (1923)

- **A page is a salesperson.** "The only purpose of advertising is to make sales" *(verified
  wording)*. Test every element with his question: "Would it help a salesman sell the goods?"
  ("Salesmanship in print" is John E. Kennedy's phrase, from 1904.)
- **Pick out the buyer.** "The purpose of the headline is to pick out people you can interest"
  *(verified wording)*.
- **Be specific.**
  - "Platitudes and generalities roll off the human understanding like water from a duck. They
    leave no impression whatever" *(verified wording)*.
  - A specific claim is either true or a lie, so readers believe it. "Actual figures are not
    generally discounted" *(verified wording)*.
  - Superlatives such as "best in the world" "are usually damaging" *(verified wording)*.
  - Don't say a lamp gives more light; say "three and one-third times" the light.
- **Tell the full story.** People who read on are interested. Give them every reason to buy.
- **Offer service, not a demand.** "The best ads ask no one to buy" *(verified wording)*. Offer
  information, a sample or a risk-free first step so the buyer can prove your claims. On the web:
  free trials, samples, demos, free tools, generous guarantees.
- **Show the bright side.** Picture the outcome, "not the dark and uninviting side of things"
  *(verified wording)*. Don't attack rivals.
- **Test.** "Almost any question can be answered, cheaply, quickly and finally, by a test campaign"
  *(verified wording)*.
- **The Schlitz lesson (the "preemptive claim").** Hopkins toured the brewery and found steps every
  brewer took:
  - filtered-air cooling rooms;
  - bottles washed four times by machinery;
  - water from deep wells;
  - pipes cleaned with live steam.

  Nobody had ever told the public. He described the process, and it became Schlitz's quality
  *(story as widely retold; exact wording and the market-share outcome not verified against the
  primary text)*. On a page: describe the work behind the result (how it's made, tested, reviewed,
  roasted, secured) specifically. It's often the most credible differentiator available, even when
  competitors do the same.

**For perceived value:** specifics and process make quality visible; samples and risk-free trials
raise perceived likelihood at almost no cost to the buyer.

## 3. Eugene Schwartz: *Breakthrough Advertising* (1966)

- **Channel desire, don't create it.** "Copy cannot create desire for a product. It can only take the
  hopes, dreams, fears and desires that already exist in the hearts of millions of people, and focus
  those already-existing desires onto a particular product" *(verified wording)*. On a page, find
  the desire in customer language and connect it to the product.
- **Awareness decides the opening.** The five levels (the modern labels are practitioners'
  shorthand) and how to open for each:

  | Level | Open with |
  |---|---|
  | Most aware | The product and the deal |
  | Product-aware | Why it's better, with new proof |
  | Solution-aware | The result |
  | Problem-aware | The problem and the promise of a solution |
  | Unaware | Identity or a story |

  The less aware the visitor, the longer and more indirect the page. (See
  `page-architecture.md` §4.)
- **Market sophistication decides the claim.**
  - **Stage 1:** say it simply.
  - **Stage 2:** enlarge the claim.
  - **Stage 3:** introduce a **mechanism**, "a new way to making the old promise work"
    *(verified wording)*.
  - **Stage 4:** elaborate the mechanism: "easier, quicker, surer" *(verified wording)*.
  - **Stage 5:** identification with the prospect over claims.
- **Techniques:**
  - **Intensification:** make the claim vivid, put the reader in the scene, show it in action,
    let them test it.
  - **Gradualization:** a staircase of beliefs the reader already accepts.
  - **Mechanization:** explain how it works.

**For perceived value:** a clear mechanism makes a familiar promise believable again; starting
where the visitor is removes the feeling of being sold to; intensification makes the benefit feel
bigger because it feels real.

## 4. John Caples: *Tested Advertising Methods* (1932)

- "If the headline is poor, the copy will not be read" *(verified wording)*.
- Headlines appeal to **self-interest**, carry **news**, or arouse **curiosity**. The first two work
  best; "the effectiveness of the average curiosity headline is doubtful" *(verified wording)*.
  Curiosity helps only on top of self-interest or news.
- **Test the appeal, not only the wording:** he saw one ad sell "19½ times as much as another" in the
  same space and publication *(verified wording)*.
- **Transformation stories** work. "They Laughed When I Sat Down At the Piano But When I Started to
  Play!" (1926) is a before/after with social stakes.

## 5. Sugarman, Halbert, Collier

- **Joseph Sugarman** (*The Adweek Copywriting Handbook*):
  - The "slippery slide": the headline's job is to get the first sentence read; the first
    sentence's job is to get the second read. Short opening sentences.
  - Of his psychological triggers, the value-perception ones are **proof of value** and **justify
    the purchase** (the rational alibi for an emotional decision, such as cost per use or a
    comparison with the alternative), and **satisfaction conviction** (a guarantee so strong it
    signals confidence).
- **Gary Halbert** (*The Boron Letters*): the one advantage he'd want selling hamburgers is "A
  STARVING CROWD!" *(verified wording)*. Market beats copy: traffic that doesn't want the outcome
  can't be copywritten into buying. Say so when an audit finds the page is fine and the traffic is
  the problem.
- **Robert Collier** (*The Robert Collier Letter Book*, 1931): enter the conversation already taking
  place in the reader's mind *(paraphrase; the common wording isn't confirmed as his)*. Open with
  what they're already thinking, in their words.

## 6. Rory Sutherland: *Alchemy* (2019)

The key source on value *perception*.

- **All value is perceived.** "A flower is simply a weed with an advertising budget" *(verified
  wording)*. Changing how something is perceived can add as much value as changing the thing.
- **Psychological moonshots** *(paraphrase)*: cheap changes to perception can do what expensive
  changes to reality do.
  - Uber's map doesn't make the car arrive sooner, but watching it approach makes the wait
    bearable.
  - He argued that a fraction of Eurostar's £6bn spent on making the journey pleasant would have
    beaten shaving 40 minutes off it.

  On a page: make waiting, onboarding, risk and effort *feel* smaller. Show what happens after the
  click, the progress, the timeline. Don't only list features.
- **Costly signals are believed.** "In nature, as in business, an expensive signal is a reliable
  signal" *(verified wording)*. Things that are costly to fake signal commitment:
  - a strong guarantee;
  - production quality;
  - a named founder with a real address;
  - detailed, long-form proof;
  - generous free value.

  A page that looks cheap signals a cheap product.
- **Psycho-logic.** "The human mind does not run on logic any more than a horse runs on petrol"
  *(verified wording, widely quoted)*. His "four S's": signalling, subconscious hacking (framing),
  satisficing, psychophysics.
- **Price and packaging are part of the product.** Red Bull's high price, small can and odd taste
  signalled potency. Underpricing can lower perceived efficacy (see placebo pricing, §10).
- Useful rules from the book: "The opposite of a good idea can also be a good idea"; "Don't design
  for average"; "It doesn't pay to be logical if everyone else is being logical"; "Dare to be
  trivial" *(verified wording, from his 11 rules)*.

**For perceived value:** value lives in the frame and the signals. Choose what the product is
compared with; shrink felt costs (time, effort, risk) and enlarge felt gains; send signals that
would be expensive to fake.

## 7. Positioning: Ries & Trout, April Dunford

- **Ries & Trout** (*Positioning*, 1981): "Positioning is not what you do to a product. Positioning
  is what you do to the mind of the prospect" *(verified wording)*. Own one idea. In a crowded
  category, make a new one.
- **April Dunford** (*Obviously Awesome*, 2019): positioning defines how a product is the best in
  the world at delivering some value that a well-defined set of customers cares a lot about
  *(close to verified wording)*. It has five components, in order:
  1. **Competitive alternatives:** what customers would do without you, including spreadsheets,
     hiring someone, or nothing.
  2. **Unique attributes:** what you have that the alternatives lack.
  3. **Value (and proof):** what those attributes let customers do.
  4. **Best-fit customers:** who cares most about that value.
  5. **Market category:** the frame that makes the value obvious.
- **Context makes value obvious.** Customers use contextual clues to work out what a product is, who
  it's for and why they should care. The same baked good sold as a "muffin" rather than a "cake"
  changes the competitors, the price and the buyers.

**For perceived value:** value is always relative. The alternatives you set up, and the category
you name, decide what price looks reasonable. A $49/month rota app framed against the manager's
three hours of Sunday spreadsheet work feels different from the same app framed against free spreadsheets.

## 8. Alex Hormozi: *$100M Offers* (2021)

A practitioner framework from his own businesses, not controlled research [PO]. It is useful as a
checklist.

- **Value equation:** value = (dream outcome × perceived likelihood of achievement) ÷ (time delay ×
  effort & sacrifice) *(verified wording)*. This is the skill's value ledger. See `value-perception.md`.
- **Grand Slam Offer:** an offer "so good people feel stupid saying no" *(verified wording)*.
  1. List every obstacle the buyer faces.
  2. Turn each obstacle into a solution.
  3. Trim the costly, low-value items and stack the cheap, high-value ones.

  The aim is to widen the gap between value and price instead of cutting price.
- **Enhancements:** scarcity, urgency, bonuses, guarantees (unconditional, conditional,
  anti-guarantee, implied) and naming. On a landing page, scarcity and urgency are allowed only
  when real (`proof-and-compliance.md` §5). Assigning dollar values to stack items is where
  offers turn deceptive. Use only real prices, or describe the value in outcomes instead.

## 9. Robert Cialdini: *Influence* (1984), *Pre-Suasion* (2016)

The seven principles, and their honest use on a page:

| Principle | Honest use on a page |
|---|---|
| **Reciprocity** | Free tools, samples, useful content. |
| **Commitment / consistency** | A small first step: a free tier, a quiz, a sample. |
| **Social proof** | Real counts, reviews and logos, ideally from customers like the reader. |
| **Authority** | Real credentials, certifications, press. |
| **Liking** | A human tone, the founder story. |
| **Scarcity** | Only when true. |
| **Unity** | Shared identity: "for designers who…". |

Cialdini separates the *detective*, who finds real scarcity or proof and states it honestly, from
the *smuggler*, who counterfeits it *(paraphrase)*. Fake timers, fake stock and fake reviews are
smuggling.

Scarcity evidence: identical cookies were rated more desirable from a jar of 2 than a jar of 10,
and more still when the supply had just dropped (Worchel, Lee & Adewole 1975, [PR]).

## 10. Behavioural economics: what holds up

| Effect | Status | Use on a page |
|---|---|---|
| **Anchoring** (Tversky & Kahneman 1974) | Robust in general (Many Labs replication, 2014). *Arbitrary* anchors on willingness to pay failed replication (Maniadis, Tufano & List 2014, *AER*). | Anchor on *relevant, true* references: the cost of the alternative, the cost of the problem, a genuine higher tier. |
| **Framing** (Tversky & Kahneman 1981; Levin & Gaeth 1988) | Robust; the effect shrinks after experience. | Frame honestly: overpromising turns into disappointment and refunds. |
| **Compromise effect** (Simonson 1989, [PR]) | Solid. | A middle tier gains share; highlight the plan most customers should pick. |
| **Decoy / attraction** (Huber, Payne & Puto 1982) | Contested: weak or absent with realistic stimuli (Frederick, Lee & Baskin 2014). Ariely's *Economist* example is a classroom demonstration, and one of Ariely's co-authored papers was retracted in 2021. | Design real tiers; test before believing any lift. |
| **Charm prices** (Anderson & Simester 2003, [PR] field experiments) | Real for value retail, weaker next to "Sale" cues, works when the left digit changes (Thomas & Morwitz 2005). **Round prices** suit premium and emotional purchases (Wadhwa & Zhang 2015; Stiving 2000). | Match the ending to the positioning. |
| **Loss aversion** | Debated as a general law (Gal & Rucker 2018 vs Mrkva et al. 2020); weak for small stakes. | Loss framing ("you're losing $X a month to no-shows") fits larger, concrete losses. Don't lean on it for small purchases. |
| **Price as a quality cue** (Rao & Monroe 1989 meta-analysis, [PR]) | Moderate and reliable, strongest when quality is hard to judge upfront. | Deep discounts can cheapen perceived quality. Prefer adding value to cutting price. |
| **Placebo pricing** (Shiv, Carmon & Ariely 2005; Waber et al. 2008; Plassmann et al. 2008) | Small samples, conceptually replicated; hold moderately. | What people pay shapes what they experience. Another reason to avoid reflexive discounting. |
| **Pennies-a-day** (Gourville 1998, [PR]) | Solid. | An honest per-day or per-use frame makes an aggregate price feel smaller. Keep the billed total visible. |
| **Processing fluency** (Reber & Schwarz 1999; Alter & Oppenheimer 2006, [PR]) | Solid. | Easy-to-read statements are judged more true. Plain words, clean type, short sentences. |
| **Needless complexity** (Oppenheimer 2006, [PR]) | Solid. | Big words make the writer seem *less* intelligent. |
| **Photos and truthiness** (Newman et al. 2012, [PR]) | Solid. | Real photos make claims feel true. Use real ones only. |

## 11. Where the sources disagree, and how to resolve it

1. **Long vs short copy.** Length should grow as awareness falls and as price and risk rise
   (Schwartz). Ogilvy's own data says only that readership barely drops between 50 and 500 words:
   length must be earned by relevance. A returning visitor buying a $9 add-on needs the offer; a
   cold visitor facing a $2,000 decision needs the argument.
2. **Clarity vs cleverness.** Ogilvy, Hopkins and Caples want plain benefit headlines. Sutherland
   prizes the counterintuitive idea. Be counterintuitive in *strategy* (the frame, the offer, the
   signal) and plain in *sentences*.
3. **Bright side vs agitation.** Hopkins says show the outcome; PAS opens with pain. Name the
   problem briefly in the reader's own words, then spend most of the page on the outcome.
4. **Facts vs signals.** Hopkins and Ogilvy want facts; Sutherland says perception and signals rule.
   Specific facts *are* costly signals, because they can be checked. Schlitz is Sutherland's point
   made by Hopkins.
5. **Brand vs direct response.** Ogilvy held both: every page builds the brand *and* should be
   measured. Don't win this month's conversion with tactics that cheapen the brand's price
   perception.
6. **Scarcity works and is dangerous.** Use only what's real (Cialdini's detective, not the smuggler).
7. **Market beats copy** (Halbert, Schwartz). If the traffic doesn't want the outcome, no rewrite
   will fix conversion. Say so.

## 12. Folklore to avoid repeating

- The "8-second attention span" (no credible source) and Unbounce's "47-second attention span"
  (screen-switching data, not attention capacity).
- "The fold doesn't matter" and "people don't scroll". Both are wrong: they scroll if the top earns it.
- "Design for the F-pattern". It's a symptom of wall-of-text formatting.
- "Red buttons convert better". That was a single test won by contrast.
- "Amazon loses 1% of sales per 100 ms". A 2006 anecdote from a talk.
- Ariely's *Economist* decoy as proof, and social-security-number anchoring on willingness to pay.
- Loss aversion as a fixed "2:1" law.
- "If it doesn't sell, it isn't creative" as Ogilvy's own line (it's Benton & Bowles's), and
  "salesmanship in print" as Hopkins's (it's Kennedy's).
- Unsourced round-number stats from SEO blogs ("'Starting at $X' converts 22% better",
  "annual discount → 31% more annual plans"). If you can't name the primary source, don't use it.
