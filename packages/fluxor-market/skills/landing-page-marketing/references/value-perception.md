# Value perception

How to find the value in a product and make visitors feel it, honestly. This is the thinking behind
the value brief. The rubric in `audit-rubric.md` scores the same levers.

## 1. The model

A visitor buys when the value they perceive is clearly bigger than the cost they perceive, compared
with their best alternative. Perceived value has parts, and a page can work on each one:

| Lever | Visitor's question | Page raises it with |
|---|---|---|
| **Dream outcome** | "What will my life/work look like after?" | A specific, vivid result in their words; the before → after |
| **Perceived likelihood** | "Will it actually work, for someone like me?" | Proof next to each claim; mechanism; demonstration; matched testimonials |
| **Time to value** | "How soon do I get something?" | First-win time; steps; timeline; instant access |
| **Effort & sacrifice** | "What will I have to do, learn, switch or give up?" | "How it works" in 3 steps; "no X needed"; imports and integrations; short forms |
| **Risk** | "What if it doesn't work?" | Trial, guarantee, cancel terms, security, transparency |
| **Price frame** | "Is this a lot or a little?" | An honest anchor: the real alternative, the cost of the problem, per-use cost |
| **Differentiation** | "Why this, not the other thing?" | Unique attributes → value → proof, against named alternatives |

The first five come from Hormozi's value equation (outcome × likelihood ÷ time × effort) plus risk,
which he handles through guarantees. The last two come from positioning (Dunford: value only exists
relative to alternatives) and pricing psychology (anchoring). They multiply rather than add. A
great outcome with zero believability is worth zero, which is why proof carries the most weight in
the rubric.

Two further ideas shape how the levers are delivered:

- **Perception is changeable without changing the product** (Sutherland). The same wait feels
  shorter when you can see progress. The same price feels smaller next to a bigger, relevant
  number. The same quality feels higher when the process behind it is described (Hopkins's
  Schlitz).
- **Specific facts are the most trusted way to raise perception** (Hopkins, Ogilvy). They can be
  checked, so they're believed. Adjectives can't be checked, so they're discounted.

## 2. Finding the value

### Product facts

Before writing, list what's true and checkable:

- what it does;
- numbers (results, speed, counts, prices);
- how it works;
- what's included;
- who uses it;
- certifications;
- guarantees;
- the process behind it (how it's made, tested, reviewed).

Give each an ID and a source. Many pages undersell because the team stopped seeing facts that would
impress an outsider, like the brewery that never mentioned its steam-cleaned pipes.

Questions that surface hidden value:

- What do you do that customers never see? (process, testing, curation, human review)
- What happens in the first 5 minutes / first day / first week of using it?
- What's the most impressive *typical* result? What's the metric behind it?
- What did customers use before, and what did it cost them?
- What do customers say when they recommend it to a friend?
- What makes people hesitate, and what's the truthful answer?
- What would you have to be very confident to offer (a guarantee, a free month, done-for-you setup)?

### Customer language (voice of the customer)

Customers' own words make the best headlines and section titles, because they are the conversation
already in the reader's mind (Collier) and the desire that copy can only channel (Schwartz).

Where to look:

- the product's reviews (G2, Capterra, App Store/Google Play, Amazon, Google, Trustpilot);
- **competitors' reviews** (3-star reviews are gold: they say what's missing);
- Reddit and forums;
- support tickets;
- sales-call notes;
- onboarding surveys ("what almost stopped you?");
- testimonials the user already has;
- the search queries that bring traffic.

How to mine:

1. Collect 20–100 snippets verbatim, with source.
2. Tag each one: pain · desired outcome · objection/anxiety · trigger moment · reason chosen ·
   alternative used before.
3. Count repeats. The most frequent pain and outcome, in the most vivid phrasing, drive the
   headline and the problem section. Recurring objections become FAQ entries and proof placements.
4. Keep exact phrasing ("texting everyone at 11pm to cover a shift", "the rota spreadsheet"). Don't
   polish it into marketing language.

If you can't reach any sources, write from facts and mark `[VOC: …]` where customer phrasing
should replace yours. Tell the user where to get it.

### Alternatives and the cost of the problem

List what the buyer would do without the product, and what each costs in money, time, effort,
risk and frustration:

- a competitor;
- a manual workaround (spreadsheets, email);
- hiring someone;
- a cheaper, worse option;
- doing nothing.

This gives you three things:

- **Differentiation:** the attributes you have that the alternatives lack.
- **The honest anchor:** the price of the alternative, or the cost of the problem.
- **The problem section:** the cost of the status quo, in their words.

Compute the cost of the problem only from the user's facts or clearly labelled assumptions: "If
3 clients pay 20 days late each month…". Never state an assumption as a fact.

## 3. The levers, one by one

### Dream outcome

- Name the result, not the activity: "Every shift covered", not "Workforce management". "Run
  without blisters", not "Technical sock construction".
- Make it concrete enough to picture. Harry Dry's test: *can I visualise it? Can I falsify it? Can
  nobody else say this?* If the answer to all three is no, rewrite.
- Use the before → after. The before in their words (brief), the after vivid and specific.
- Match the outcome to the buyer's real goal. People taking a sourdough course want *bread their
  family asks for*, not to "understand fermentation".
- Don't promise more than the typical customer gets. Overpromising raises value for one visit and
  destroys it at the first experience (and invites refunds and complaints).

### Perceived likelihood

The multiplier. Without it the outcome is worth nothing.

- Put proof **next to** each claim, not in a separate testimonial wall.
- Proof types, strongest first: demonstration, specific attributed results, matched named
  testimonials, third-party ratings with counts, logos and cases, credentials, mechanism,
  guarantee (see `proof-and-compliance.md` §2).
- **Mechanism:** a one-line, checkable reason it works ("reminders go out on a schedule you set, so
  nobody has to remember"). It matters most in crowded markets (Schwartz stage 3+).
- **Similarity:** proof from people like the reader (same role, size, situation) counts most.
- **"Will it work for *me*?"**: address the specific worry ("even if you've never coded",
  "works with your existing Stripe account").

### Time to value

- State the time to the *first* win, which should be short and true: "first rota in about 10
  minutes", "arrives in 3 working days", "bake your first loaf this weekend".
- Separate it from the time to the *full* outcome, and be honest about that: "most restaurants cut
  scheduling time in half within the first month".
- Show the path: 3 steps, a timeline, what happens after the click. Seeing progress makes waiting
  feel shorter (Sutherland's Uber map).
- Make the CTA itself deliver an immediate win where possible ("Build my first rota free").

### Effort & sacrifice

- Show how little the buyer has to do: steps, defaults, done-for-you parts.
- Remove switching costs explicitly: imports, integrations with tools they already use, "keeps
  your existing X", migration help.
- Name what they'll *stop* doing (the chore disappears), which is often more vivid than what they'll
  gain.
- Shorten the first step: an email to start, no card for a trial, no sales call for a self-serve
  product. A long form on a low-price product is a hidden price.
- Sacrifices aren't only effort: status, identity ("I'm not a tech person"), control ("will it
  email my clients without asking?"). Answer those too.

### Risk

- State the terms that make trying safe, right beside the CTA: free trial length, card or no card,
  cancel anytime and how, refund window, free tier.
- A **guarantee** is also a costly signal of confidence (Sutherland; Sugarman's "satisfaction
  conviction"). Research on lenient return policies finds they raise purchases more than returns
  (Janakiraman et al. 2016 meta-analysis, [PR]). Guarantees are a business decision: **propose
  one, never invent one.** Say what it could be ("30-day money-back", "if you don't get paid faster
  in 60 days, we refund you") and ask whether they'll honour it.
- Answer the specific fears: data security (exact certifications), lock-in (export), quality
  (returns), wasted time (a short first lesson, a sample).
- Transparency lowers risk: visible price, real company details, real people, honest limitations
  ("not for teams over 10").

### Price frame

See §4.

### Differentiation

- Only attributes the alternatives lack count. "Easy to use" is not differentiation.
- For each: attribute → so what (value) → proof.
- Pick the comparison that makes those attributes decisive (Dunford's context). Framing against
  "three manager hours every Sunday" or "a WhatsApp group of shift swaps" creates a different price
  expectation than framing against "invoicing software".
- Say who it's *not* for. It sharpens the fit for the right buyer and reads as honest.

## 4. Framing the price

Price is never judged alone. It's judged against an anchor. Choose an honest one, and do the math
visibly.

- **The real alternative:** "less than two hours of a manager's time a month", "a physio visit
  costs €45; a pair of these costs €22". Use only real, relevant alternatives with checkable
  numbers. Anchoring on relevant references is robust; arbitrary anchors are not (Maniadis et al.
  2014).
- **The cost of the problem:** "one no-show shift you don't have to cover yourself pays for a month". Base it on
  the user's facts or labelled, conservative assumptions.
- **Unit framing:** per month, per day, per cup, per lesson, per seat (Gourville's
  "pennies-a-day", [PR]). Keep the billed total visible next to it, or it reads as a trick.
- **Tiers:**
  - Name plans by who they're for.
  - Express differences as outcomes.
  - Highlight the plan most customers should choose (the compromise effect, Simonson 1989).
  - Every tier must be a genuine choice. Decoy tiers are fragile and can feel manipulative.
- **Annual vs monthly:** show the per-month equivalent and the real saving; keep the billed amount
  visible.
- **Show prices.** Hidden prices raise distrust. B2B visitors want them too (NN/g). For custom
  pricing, give "from" prices or typical scenarios.
- **Discounts:** deep discounts attract price-sensitive customers and can cheapen perceived
  quality (price-quality cue: Rao & Monroe 1989; placebo pricing: Shiv et al. 2005). Prefer
  adding value (a bonus, onboarding help, a longer trial) to cutting price. A discount needs an
  honest reference price and a real end date.
- **Price endings:** .99/.9 endings help value-oriented retail; round prices suit premium and
  emotional purchases. Match the positioning.
- **Put the price after the value** on long pages, so it's read against the outcome, but make it
  findable (a "Pricing" link) for people who look for it first.

## 5. Designing the offer

The offer is what the visitor gets for the price. Improving it is often the fastest way to raise
perceived value, and it's a business decision, so propose it; don't invent it.

1. **List the buyer's obstacles** between them and the outcome: setup, learning, migration, time,
   fear of doing it wrong, getting buy-in.
2. **Turn obstacles into offer components** the business could really provide: templates, an import
   service, a quickstart call, a checklist, a community, a review, a longer trial.
3. **Stack what's real.** List everything genuinely included, described by the outcome each part
   delivers. Don't assign made-up dollar values. If you show values, they must be what someone
   actually pays for that item separately.
4. **Reverse the risk** with terms the business will honour (§3 Risk).
5. **Use real urgency, or none.** A cohort start date, a launch price with an end date, real
   capacity limits. Otherwise lower the cost of acting now instead.
6. **Name it** so it's easy to remember and says what it is ("The Get-Paid Plan", "Fresh-Roast
   Monthly"). Optional.

Present offer suggestions to the user as options with the trade-off. Don't put them on the page
until they're confirmed.

## 6. Signals and specificity

- **Specific > general.** Every claim should contain a number, a name, a time, a place or a
  mechanism. "3⅓ times the light", not "more light" (Hopkins).
- **Costly signals** (Sutherland) are hard to fake, so they're believed:
  - a strong guarantee;
  - a named founder and real address;
  - detailed process descriptions;
  - long, specific proof;
  - production quality;
  - free value given upfront;
  - honest limitations.
- **Process as proof** (Hopkins's Schlitz): describe the work behind the result: roasted every
  Tuesday, reviewed by a person, tested on 40 devices, encrypted at rest in Frankfurt. Even when
  competitors do the same, the one who says it owns it.
- **Design quality is a signal.** A page that looks cheap signals a cheap product (Stanford
  credibility research: "design look" was the most cited credibility factor).
- **Consistency is a signal.** Price, terms and claims identical across hero, pricing, FAQ and
  checkout.

## 7. Worked example (value ledger)

A GPS dog collar, "Trailtag", $129 + $5/month. From a founder's notes:

- 14-day battery;
- live location every 3 seconds in "lost dog mode";
- works on LTE-M in 30 countries;
- escape alerts when the dog leaves a drawn boundary;
- 4.6★ from 2,400 reviews;
- 60-day returns.

Customers' words: "the panic when she slipped the leash", "I don't want another thing to charge
every night".

| Lever | Weak version | Strong version (all from facts) |
|---|---|---|
| Outcome | "Smart GPS tracking for pets" | "Know where your dog is within seconds, even if they bolt" |
| Likelihood | "Accurate and reliable" | "Live location every 3 seconds in lost-dog mode · 4.6★ from 2,400 owners" plus a named review about a real escape |
| Time | — | "Alerts the moment they cross the line you draw" |
| Effort | "Easy to use" | "Charge it every two weeks, not every night" (answers the verbatim objection) |
| Risk | — | "60-day returns. If it doesn't fit your dog or your life, send it back." |
| Price frame | "$129 + $5/mo" | "$5 a month, less than a bag of treats, for knowing where they are" (the anchor is honest only if true in the market; otherwise use a plain per-day frame) |
| Differentiation | "Best-in-class tracker" | "Works on cellular in 30 countries, not just Bluetooth range like tag trackers" |

## 8. Common ways pages leak value

- **Feature lists without outcomes:** the reader has to do the translation, and won't.
- **Company-centred copy** ("we are passionate…", "our mission"): the reader cares about their
  problem.
- **Adjectives instead of proof:** "powerful", "seamless", "world-class" are discounted to zero.
- **Proof far from claims:** testimonials walled off at the bottom.
- **Anonymous or vague proof:** "Happy customer" reads as invented.
- **Hidden price or hidden terms:** raises perceived risk; visitors assume the worst.
- **A CTA that asks too much for this product** (a demo form for a $12 tool): the effort costs more
  than the price.
- **Fake urgency:** undermines every true claim on the page once noticed.
- **Mismatch with the traffic:** the ad promised one thing, the page talks about another.
- **Looking cheap or broken on mobile:** a quality signal, working against you.
- **Discounting instead of adding value:** trains buyers to see the product as worth less.
